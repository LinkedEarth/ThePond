Metadata for Reuse

Home › LilyPads › Data › 2. Metadata for Reuse

A data file is not necessarily usable just because it opens. Metadata (information about the data) provide the context needed to interpret, evaluate, reuse, and share the data correctly. In this LilyPad, you’ll discover which metadata you need by trying to use a dataset without them.

1. Data Usability

About 15 minutes

Imagine that you work for a city office that checks whether schools meet lead (Pb) exposure standards. A testing company has just sampled the soil in the playground at MADISON ELEM., and a colleague forwards you their file:

Here’s the soil data from Madison. Can you tell me whether the playground is above the 400 ppm limit for children’s play areas?

(ppm means parts per million: 400 ppm is 400 milligrams of lead per kilogram of soil.)

This is the file, madison_soil.csv, exactly as you received it:

id,loc,d,Pb,m,f
M-01,road,1,610,12,1
M-02,road,6,380,15,1
M-03,road,15,99,18,0
M-04,sandbox,1,99,10,1
M-05,sandbox,6,70,14,1
M-06,wall,1,360,20,1
M-07,wall,6,730,22,0

Fictional school, company and values, created for The Pond (2026).

It opens without any trouble. Let’s see whether you can use it.

1.1 Where did this file come from?

Before you start, think about the file itself. What would you want to know about where it came from? Select as many as you like, then reveal our answer.

A company you know, a student project, or an unknown source? Knowing who did the work offers clue as to their likely practices and conventions (though not always!).

Is this really MADISON ELEM., and this year’s sampling? The file names no school and no date.

From the company itself, or from somewhere else? An original from the source is easier to check than a copy of a copy.

Was anything removed, renamed, or recalculated before it reached you?

Irrelevant: the file size tells you nothing about where the data came from.

Irrelevant: any spreadsheet or text editor opens a CSV (comma-separated values) file the same way.

What your colleague tells you: The samples were collected at MADISON ELEM. on October 14, 2026, by the field crew of Northgate Soil Testing, a company that the city has a contract with. Your colleague downloaded the file from the company’s client portal and didn’t change it.

This information about where data came from, and what happened to them along the way, is called provenance. It doesn’t make the data trustworthy on its own, but it gives you what you need to evaluate the data. You’ll look at provenance in more depth in Data Quality and Provenance.

Key takeaway 1.1

Before using a dataset, find out who produced it, where you got it, and whether anything changed along the way. This provenance doesn’t make data trustworthy, but without it you can’t judge whether they are.

1.2 How deep is each sample?

Lead tends to build up near the surface, which is also the soil that children touch. So depth matters.

Your turn! Which column do you think contains the depth?

Now assume that d is the depth. How deep is sample M-02, in meters?

You can type a number, but can you be sure it’s right? What would you need to know?

The column names (d, m) are too short to be sure.

Is 6 a depth in millimeters, centimeters, inches, or feet? Each gives a very different sample.

From the ground surface, or from the top of a layer of mulch or sand? To the top, middle, or bottom of the sample?

Not a first choice: the type of lead analyzer says little about how depth was recorded.

Irrelevant here: the time of day doesn’t change how deep a sample was.

You ask the testing company, which replies: d is the depth below the ground surface to the middle of the sample, in inches.

Key takeaway 1.2

Raw values are unusable without ancillary information, known as metadata. When you archive your data, make sure you include all the information necessary to use them, from the standpoint of a reasonable person who does not live in your head.

1.3 Missing Values

Before you average anything, you need to remove the missing values: samples for which there is no lead measurement.

Your turn! Drop the rows that you think have no lead measurement, and watch the average change.

id loc d Pb m f
M-01 road 1 610 12 1
M-02 road 6 380 15 1
M-03 road 15 99 18 0
M-04 sandbox 1 99 10 1
M-05 sandbox 6 70 14 1
M-06 wall 1 360 20 1
M-07 wall 6 730 22 0

Did you drop any? Nothing in the file says that a value is missing. What would you need to know?

Some files leave a blank, others use a special code such as -999 or 99.

Not analyzed? Sample lost? Below what the instrument can detect? Each has different implications.

Irrelevant: lead in soil can range from a few ppm to many thousands. No value in this file is impossible.

You ask the testing company, which replies: a lead value of 99 means that the sample was not analyzed. That’s M-03 only: lead at M-04 was measured, and really is 99 ppm.

Here is what each choice does to the average of all the samples:

Your choice Average lead Compared with the 400 ppm limit
Keep every row (the raw average) 335 ppm Below
Drop M-03 only (correct) 375 ppm Below
Drop both 99s 430 ppm Above

This is a consequential number. An average above 400 ppm could close the playground, alarm parents, and trigger an expensive cleanup. Here, deleting one real measurement, because an undocumented code looked like it, would have done exactly that. (This isn’t the final answer yet: quality control and dry soil will change the picture again in 1.4 and 1.5.)

Lead at 99 ppm is a perfectly ordinary value, so 99 was a particularly unwise choice to encode missingness: nothing in the numbers can tell the two rows apart: only the metadata can.

What should 99 have been?

Your turn! Pick the best way to record “no value here,” then reveal our take on each option.

The worst choice: 0 is a real and reassuring value (“no lead at all”). A sample that was never analyzed would look like the cleanest soil in the playground.

Impossible for a concentration, so a person would spot it. But software doesn’t know that: unless told otherwise, it averages −999 in with the real values, so that an average of a 1000 with this -999 would come out close to 0: a very troublesome false negative.

Ambiguous: was the value not measured, forgotten, or deleted by accident? Software also disagrees: some tools skip empty cells, others treat them as 0.

Short for “not available.” It’s widely used, but it’s just text: whether it’s read as “missing” depends on the software.

“Not a number,” a special value defined by the IEEE 754 standard for how computers do arithmetic. Nearly all numerical software recognizes it, and it can never be mistaken for a measurement. Arithmetic with NaN gives NaN (1 + NaN = NaN), so a missing value can’t sneak into a calculation as a number: tools either skip it or tell you it’s there.

NaN isn’t perfect either
  • In a CSV file, NaN is just three letters. Python’s pandas and R read it as a number; a spreadsheet may show it as text.
  • NaN is not equal to anything, including itself. A test such as x == NaN is always false, so software provides dedicated checks (isnan, is.nan).
  • Not all analysis code can deal with NaNs. For instance, trying to estimate the naive average of all these measurements would produce a NaN. However, most software worth its salt can handle that (e.g. NumPy).
  • It doesn’t say why the value is missing. Not analyzed, sample lost, failed quality control, withheld for privacy: each might call for a different decision. Record the reason in a separate column, and say what it means in the metadata.

Also, not every gap is missing data. A lab may report <10: lead is below the detection limit of 10 ppm, the lowest level its instrument can measure reliably. That’s information (the value is less than 10 ppm), and replacing it with NaN would throw it away.

Key takeaway 1.3

Even a simple data cleaning step such as removing missing values hinges on metadata. Before you share data, record missing values in a way that can’t be mistaken for a measurement (such as NaN), flag why a value is reported missing, and document both conventions.

1.4 Quality Control

Testing companies check their measurements before releasing them. This is called quality control (QC). The column f looks like a QC flag.

Your turn! To keep only the measurements that passed QC, which rows would you keep?

What would you need to know?

Does 1 mean “passed” or “problem”? Conventions differ between companies and fields.

Useful to know, but not needed to filter the table: the decision has already been made.

Irrelevant: the average of a set of codes means nothing.

You ask the testing company, which replies: f is the QC flag for lead only: 1 = passed, 0 = failed. Depth and moisture are not flagged.

So you keep M-01, M-02, M-04, M-05 and M-06. M-07’s 730 ppm, the highest value in the file, failed QC. M-03 failed too: it was never analyzed.

Metadata can be specific to a single variable: here the flag tells you about lead, not about the rest of the row. In Different Types of Data, you met a similar QC/status column; there, its meaning was written out in words.

Key takeaway 1.4

Codes such as 0 and 1 mean nothing on their own. The metadata must say what each value stands for, and which variable it applies to.

1.5 Dry soil, or wet soil?

Look back at how we defined ppm at the start: “400 milligrams of lead per kilogram of soil.” We left out a consequential detail: the limit applies to lead per kilogram of dry soil.

Your turn! Which of the surface samples (d = 1) are above the 400 ppm limit?

This one requires knowledge specific to soil science. What would you need to know?

Labs can report lead per kilogram of soil as received (water included) or per kilogram of dried soil. Wet soil is partly water, so the same soil gives a lower number when measured wet.

If m is the water content, you can use it to convert from wet to dry soil. But as a percentage of what?

Rain may explain why the soil was wet, but you need the measured water content, not the weather.

Irrelevant: it doesn’t change what the numbers mean.

You ask the testing company, which replies: lead (Pb) is measured in ppm on soil as received, without drying. m is the water content, as a percentage of the wet sample’s mass.

So lead per kilogram of dry soil is Pb / (1 − m/100):

id loc Pb, as received m Pb, dry soil
M-01 road 610 12 693
M-04 sandbox 99 10 110
M-06 wall 360 20 450

M-01 and M-06 are above 400 ppm.

This is the kind of metadata that a generic checklist (“include units”) would never catch: the units were ppm all along. Each discipline has its own definitions that change what a number means. In Data Quality and Provenance, you’ll see how preparing a sample (drying, sieving) affects the measurement itself.

Key takeaway 1.5

Some metadata are specific to a discipline, like whether lead was measured in wet or dry soil. No generic checklist will catch them: ask yourself what a specialist in your field would need to know to use your numbers.

1.6 Can you share it?

You now have a clean dataset. Your office wants to post it in a public data repository, and to include it in a report to denizens of the town.

Your turn! Are you allowed to?

What would you need to know?

A license states what others may do with the data: copy, modify, redistribute, and under what conditions (for instance, whether they must credit the creators).

A contract, a privacy rule, or a data use agreement can restrict sharing, or require it. Here, the city’s contract decides which license applies.

Irrelevant: you already have it. Having access to data is not the same as having permission to share them.

Not enough: an informal “go ahead” is easily forgotten or misremembered, and may not be your colleague’s to give.

The file says nothing, so you can’t assume you’re allowed. Your office checks the city’s contract with Northgate Soil Testing: it requires all data delivered to the city to be released under an open license. Northgate adds the license to the dataset’s metadata:

License: CC0 1.0 (public domain). Anyone may copy, adapt, and share the data, for any purpose, without asking permission.

CC0 asks nothing in return, not even credit. But scientific practice still expects you to cite the data you use: see Citing Data.

Requiring open licenses is common for publicly funded data. In the United States, federal guidance on open licenses asks agencies to make sure that data they obtain through contracts aren’t locked behind restrictive licenses, and encourages them to use CC0.

Permission should be written into the metadata, not left to an email or a hallway conversation, so that it travels with the data. You’ll put this into practice in Preparing Data for Sharing. To learn how licenses work, and how to choose one for your own data, dive into Licensing in the Deep End.

Key takeaway 1.6

Having data is not the same as being allowed to share them. State the license and any reuse conditions in the metadata, so that permission travels with the data.

Key takeaway 1

At every step, the file opened fine and the numbers looked ordinary. What stopped you was missing context: where the data came from, what each column means, how missing values and QC are coded, what a measurement is relative to, and what you are allowed to do with the data.

2. What metadata did you actually need?

About 4 minutes

Your turn! Think back over the exercise. What information did you need in order to trust, interpret, clean, use, and share the data?

Compare with our answer

Metadata are often grouped into two broad kinds:

Scientific metadata

Help you understand and evaluate the science:

  • variable names and definitions (d, m)
  • units (inches, ppm)
  • missing-value conventions (99)
  • QC definitions (f: 1 = passed, lead only)
  • domain-specific definitions (as received vs. dry soil)
  • methods and calibration
  • where and when the samples were taken
  • uncertainty
  • provenance: who collected the data, and what happened to them

Administrative metadata

Help you identify, manage, access, and reuse the dataset:

  • creator and contributors (Northgate Soil Testing)
  • institution (the city)
  • identifier (a name or number that points to this dataset)
  • contact information
  • license (CC0, as required by the city’s contract)
  • access restrictions
  • reuse and redistribution conditions
  • provenance: where the file was obtained, and who is responsible for it

These categories are not rigid. Provenance appears in both: it helps you interpret the science, and it tells you who is responsible for the data.

Key takeaway 2

Metadata serve different purposes. Some help researchers understand and evaluate the science, while others help identify, manage, access, and reuse the dataset.

3. From unstructured to structured metadata

About 6 minutes

3.1 Organize the metadata

Suppose that, instead of answering questions one at a time, Northgate Soil Testing had sent all of its metadata in an email:

Soil samples were collected at the MADISON ELEM. playground on October 14, 2026, by our field crew. Depth (d) is the depth below the ground surface to the middle of each sample, in inches. Lead (Pb) is in ppm, measured on the soil as received, without drying; m is the water content, as a percentage of the wet sample’s mass. A lead value of 99 means the sample was not analyzed. The flag f applies to lead only: 1 means the measurement passed quality control, 0 that it failed. As required by our contract with the city, the data are released under a CC0 license.

Everything is there, but finding one piece of information means reading the whole paragraph, and a computer can’t easily check it.

Your turn! Organize this information into a structured form: a table, a list of labeled fields, or anything else you like. There is no single correct structure.

3.2 Same information, different structures

Here is how three other people organized the same paragraph:

Person A

variable units missing
d inches —
Pb ppm (wet) 99
m % —

Person B

depth_unit: in
lead_units: ppm
lead_basis: as received
lead_missing: 99
qc_good: 1

Person C

d:
  unit: inch
Pb:
  units: mg/kg
  dry: no
  missingValue: 99
  qcFlag: f (1 = pass)

Your turn! All three are structured. Could a computer combine them automatically?

3.3 Conventions

Structured metadata are easier to find, read, and check than a paragraph. But structure alone doesn’t make them interoperable, meaning that metadata from different people and programs can work together. For that, a community needs to agree on:

  • field names: always units, never unit or depth_unit
  • required and optional fields: e.g. every dataset must have a license
  • definitions: what exactly each field means
  • allowed values and vocabularies: e.g. an agreed list of unit spellings, or of terms for “lead in soil”
  • standard representations: e.g. how to write a date, or a missing value

Such agreements are called conventions (or standards). Here is Person C’s metadata, rewritten in the style of one such convention:

Pb:
  long_name: lead in soil, as received
  units: mg kg-1
  ancillary_variables: f
f:
  flag_values: 0, 1
  flag_meanings: failed passed

The field names (long_name, units, flag_values, flag_meanings) aren’t anyone’s personal choice: they come from the convention, so any software that follows it knows how to read them.

You don’t need to learn any particular convention for this LilyPad. If you’re curious, here are a few examples:

  • CF Conventions: for climate and environmental data in netCDF files (the example above is in their style).
  • DataCite Metadata Schema: administrative metadata for citing datasets in any field.
  • PaST Thesaurus: a controlled vocabulary for describing paleoclimate measurements.

Key takeaway 3

Structured metadata are useful, but structure alone does not guarantee interoperability. Communities also need agreement about field names, definitions, allowed values, and vocabularies.
