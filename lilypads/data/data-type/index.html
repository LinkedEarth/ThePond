Different Types of Data

1. What types of data do you work with?

When we think about scientific data, we often picture a spreadsheet filled with numbers. But scientific data come in many different forms.

A climate scientist might work with temperature measurements, satellite images, and climate model outputs. A geologist might use field notes, maps, and photographs of rock samples. A biologist might work with DNA sequences, microscope images, or observations of animal behavior.

All of these contain scientific information, but they represent that information in very different ways.

Text: The notebook contains written observations, descriptions, and measurements. Much of the scientific information is conveyed through language.

Image: We're looking at a photograph of handwritten notes, including a drawing. The photograph preserves visual information such as the handwriting, annotations, and layout.

Spatial: The sketch conveys spatial relationships, including where the sample was collected relative to the stream and its banks. It isn't a georeferenced map, but it still contains spatial information.

Our suggested descriptions:

Tabular Time series

Tabular: The information is organized into rows and columns. Each row represents an observation, while the columns identify the date and measured temperature.

Dataset C — Ocean temperature from a climate model

A climate researcher analyzes monthly ocean temperatures from a model. The dataset contains temperature values organized along four dimensions:

  • Time: 12 months
  • Depth: 20 levels
  • Latitude: 30 locations
  • Longitude: 40 locations

The figure shows one month at three selected depths. The complete dataset contains temperature values for every combination of the four dimensions.

How would you describe this dataset?

Select all the terms you think apply.

Dataset C — What did you find?

Our suggested descriptions:

Multidimensional Spatial Time series Image

Multidimensional: The temperature values are organized along four dimensions: time, depth, latitude, and longitude. To identify a particular temperature value, we need to specify a position along each dimension.

Spatial: Latitude, longitude, and depth describe where temperatures occur in the ocean. Researchers can examine geographic patterns, compare regions, or investigate how temperature varies with depth.

Time series: Because the model provides monthly values, we can follow how temperature changes through time at any particular location and depth. Each location can therefore have its own time series.

Image: Each two-dimensional grid of temperature values can also be treated as an image, where grid cells act like pixels and temperature values represent pixel intensities. This allows researchers to apply computer vision techniques, such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs), to climate data. Additional variables, depths, or time steps can be represented as channels or sequences of images, depending on the analysis.

What about tabular data? We could reorganize these measurements into a table, with columns for time, depth, latitude, longitude, and temperature. The scientific measurements would remain the same, but their organization would change. Some relationships that are explicit in a multidimensional grid would then need to be reconstructed from the table.

The important idea: The same scientific dataset can be understood in several ways. Climate model output is multidimensional, contains spatial information and time series, and can be represented as images for certain analyses. How we represent data influences the tools and methods we can use to study them.

Image Spatial Multidimensional

Image: The information is represented as a grid of pixels. Each pixel contains information that contributes to our view of the river delta.

The important idea: An image is not simply a picture. It can be a representation of quantitative scientific measurements, with spatial relationships and additional dimensions. The underlying data may contain more information than is visible in the displayed image.

Dataset E — What did you find?

Our suggested descriptions:

Tabular Spatial

Tabular: The measurements are organized into rows and columns. Each row represents a rock sample, and the columns describe its location and composition. We can easily compare quartz content between samples, sort measurements, or filter samples based on their properties.

Spatial: Each sample has a latitude and longitude. These coordinates tell us where the samples were collected, allowing us to map their locations, calculate distances, or investigate geographic patterns in mineral composition.

What about time series? Although the samples have identifiers and locations, there is no time dimension here. We cannot investigate how quartz content changes through time using these observations alone.

Do spatial data have to be maps? Not at all! A map is one way to visualize spatial information, but geographic coordinates stored in a table are also spatial data.

The important idea: Data do not need to look like a map to contain spatial information. The same dataset can be represented as a table, plotted on a map, or analyzed to identify relationships between location and measured properties.

Dataset F — What did you find?

Our suggested descriptions:

Video Image Time series Spatial Multidimensional

Video: The observations are recorded as moving footage, capturing how the flood develops. Researchers can review the sequence, pause at particular moments, and investigate changes.

Image: A video consists of a sequence of individual images (frames). Each frame contains visual information about the conditions at that moment.

Time series: The frames form a sequence through time. Researchers could extract repeated measurements, such as the position of the water's edge or an estimate of water level, to construct a numerical time series. The video itself is not already a table of water-level measurements.

Spatial: The footage captures relationships between the stream channel, water, sediment, and surrounding landscape. It contains spatial information, although converting image positions into real-world distances would require additional information about the camera and scene.

Multidimensional: The video can also be represented as an array with dimensions for image height, image width, and time, potentially including color channels. This representation is commonly used in computer vision.

Could this become tabular data? Yes! A researcher might review the footage and record observations such as the time of flood arrival, water level estimates, or the movement of debris. Those measurements could be organized into a table.

The important idea: Scientific data don't always begin as numerical measurements. Images and videos can contain valuable scientific observations. Extracting quantitative information from them, whether manually or using AI, requires interpretation and sometimes additional information or assumptions.

Different forms, different possibilities

You’ve now explored several types of scientific data. Some were organized into tables, others into grids, images, written notes, or video. Many fit more than one description.

But there’s another question worth exploring: Does the way information is organized affect what we can do with it?

For example, we could watch a video and identify when a flood arrived, or read a field notebook and extract temperature measurements. But what if we wanted to compare thousands of observations?

In the next activity, we’ll explore why data structure matters, even when humans or AI can interpret information that isn’t already organized into a table.

Part 3. Why does structure matter?

The field notes and the table describe the same monitoring campaign, but they organize the information differently.

In the field notes, the information is expressed through written descriptions. In the table, each observation follows a consistent structure: date, site, measurement, QC/status, and additional notes.

This makes it easier to filter observations, compare sites, identify missing measurements, and apply the same analysis to many records.

There is also an intermediate possibility: semi-structured data.

For example, a researcher might record information using labeled fields, without organizing every observation into the same table:

date: 2026-06-12
site: B
turbidity: 30 NTU
qc: needs review
note: first reading 42 NTU; probe fluctuating

This is more organized than free-form notes because individual pieces of information are labeled. But across many such records, the available fields, names, and organization might still vary.

We can therefore distinguish three broad ways of organizing information:

  • Unstructured: Information is not organized according to a consistent predefined data model, as with free-form notes, photographs, or recordings.
  • Semi-structured: Information has some recognizable organization, such as labeled fields, but may not follow a uniform schema.
  • Structured: Information follows a defined organization, such as a table with consistent fields, making it easier to compare, filter, and validate records systematically.

These are useful descriptions, not rigid boundaries.

But what about AI?

Modern AI tools can read field notes, interpret images, and extract information from many sources that aren’t already organized into tables.

So why bother structuring scientific data?

Consider what happened when we converted the field notes into a table. We had to decide which measurement to retain for Site B on June 12, how to represent an uncertain QC decision, and what to do when no measurement was collected.

An AI tool could help make those decisions, but it would still need to interpret the original observations. Those interpretations might be incomplete or incorrect.

Structure makes information and assumptions explicit, so that they can be reviewed, checked, and applied consistently.

Key takeaway

Data structure is not only about making information readable by computers. It is about making scientific information explicit enough to support reliable interpretation, comparison, validation, and reuse.
