Data Quality and Provenance

Home › LilyPads › Data › 3. Data Quality and Provenance

Imagine that you are a city official charged with determining whether schools in your district meet lead (Pb) exposure standards. Sampling in the district is done by various testing companies, using various instruments, each with a given nominal precision. State and city procedures mandate that each measurement be replicated at least once (preferably more). The instruments have known biases that can be corrected for.

You met this office in Metadata for Reuse, where the question was whether you had enough information to use a dataset. Here, the question is whether, given that information, the data are good enough for a particular purpose.

Lead concentrations can be reported in several units:

Unit name Abbreviation Equivalent to 400 ppm (EPA play area limit)
Parts per million ppm 400 ppm
Milligrams per kilogram mg/kg 400 mg/kg
Micrograms per gram µg/g 400 µg/g
Parts per billion ppb 400,000 ppb
Percent by weight % 0.04%

The recommended thresholds also vary by medium:

Medium Specific target type EPA threshold value
Paint / Dust Liquid/surface paint limit 90 ppm (0.009%)
Soil Bare soil, child play areas 400 ppm
Tap water Public utility action level 10 ppb (0.010 mg/L)


1. Where did this value come from?

About 15 minutes

1.1 A spreadsheet from a new employee

One day, a new employee of yours hands you a spreadsheet that looks like this:

PB-001 FAIRVIEW ELEM. 12 Yes
PB-002 LINCOLN MIDDLE 150 Yes
PB-003 ROOSEVELT HIGH 0.03 Yes
PB-004 JEFFERSON ELEM. 420 No
PB-005 WASHINGTON ELEM. 8 Yes

Your turn!

Four of the five schools are marked safe. Before you report that to the city council:

  1. How do you think the Safe? column was decided?
  2. What would you need to know to check it yourself?
Compare with our answer

Each value must be an average of replicates, but the spreadsheet doesn’t say of how many samples, in what units, or with what precision. Nor does it say what was sampled, so you can’t tell which threshold applies.

A Safe? column is not a reproducible determination: without the information behind it, you can’t check it.

Key takeaway 1.1

A derived value, such as a “Safe?” verdict, is only as trustworthy as the information needed to recompute it. As you saw in Metadata for Reuse, a number without its units, replicates and precision can’t be checked.

1.2 The same spreadsheet, from a senior employee

Now suppose that the exact same spreadsheet had come from your most senior employee, who has done this work for twenty years.

Your turn! Would you trust it more?

Why it matters

The employee’s experience is not the relevant variable here; metadata are. An experienced person may have years of precedent to justify how they do things, but also more unstated assumptions. If you are not operating with the same assumptions, their spreadsheet may lead you astray. We will come back to trust and reputation in Activity 5.

Key takeaway 1.2

Judge data by the evidence that comes with them, not by who handed them to you. Experience is no substitute for documentation.

Here is the same survey, with the information that was missing:

PB-002 LINCOLN MIDDLE Soil 2 Handheld analyzer B 150 ppm ± 15 400 ppm Yes

Two schools have changed from “Yes” to “No.” The new employee had applied the soil threshold, 400, to every row, regardless of the medium and units.

Your turn!

  1. Which stage of the data processing is reflected in what was archived?
  2. Can the processed value be traced back to the original observations?
  3. What transformations occurred?
  4. Are those transformations documented?
  5. Could another researcher reproduce the processed value?
Compare with our answer
  1. The average. Each value summarizes several replicate measurements.
  2. No. The individual replicates have not been archived, and they should be.
  3. Averaging, then comparison with a threshold. (The bias corrections mentioned at the start may have been applied too; the table doesn’t say.)
  4. Not explicitly. They can be inferred from the metadata: the reported medium tells us which units and threshold were likely used. It would be better to make this explicit.
  5. Not without the actual workflow: which replicates went into each average, which corrections were applied, and how. See What is a Workflow?

Did you spot that WASHINGTON ELEM. has only one replicate? The procedures require at least two measurements, so that “Yes” should not stand until the school is resampled.

Key takeaway 1.3

Provenance is the history of how a value became what it is: raw readings, corrections, averaging, comparisons. Processing isn’t a problem in itself; undocumented processing is. Archive the raw data and record each step, so that others can reproduce the final value.

1.4 How could we do better?

Even the better table can’t answer every question. Some of the evidence you need lives outside the numerical dataset.

Your turn! Which of these would you ask the testing companies for? Select as many as you like, then reveal what each one would tell you.

Show how each instrument’s readings were converted and corrected for its known bias, and when it was last checked.

Record errors, maintenance, and failed readings: was the analyzer working properly on the day PB-004 was measured?

Describe the conditions: was the tap at FAIRVIEW ELEM. flushed before sampling? Was the soil at JEFFERSON ELEM. wet?

Let you see how much the replicates disagree, and recompute each average yourself.

Samples with a certified lead content, measured alongside the real ones, show whether the instrument read accurately that day.

Documents exactly how replicates were averaged, corrections applied, and thresholds chosen, so anyone can rerun or check it.

Show where each sample was taken: a play area, or a flower bed by the parking lot?

Who collected each sample, when, and how it was stored and handled before analysis.

Key takeaway 1.4

Ask for the evidence behind the numbers. Calibration records, replicates, field notes and processing code let you check a value instead of taking it on faith. Many of them are the scientific metadata you met in Metadata for Reuse.

Key takeaway 1

Evidence supporting data quality may exist outside the numerical dataset itself. More metadata tends to be a good thing, but it often involves a tradeoff because it takes time and effort to maintain complete metadata records, so scientists often land on a compromise between what is ideal and what simply “clears the bar” (which is context-dependent, and might evolve over time).

2. What does this number actually mean?

About 10 minutes, plus 5 optional

In Activity 1, JEFFERSON ELEM. was reported at 420 ppm of lead in soil, just above the 400 ppm threshold. Before the city closes a playground, it’s worth asking what that number really tells us.

2.1 Precise, accurate, or both?

To check their instruments, four testing companies each measured the same reference soil five times. A reference soil is a sample whose lead content has been certified by a standards laboratory, here at 400 ppm.

Your turn! For each analyzer, decide whether its readings are precise and whether they are accurate.

In each plot, the dots are the five readings and the triangle marks their average. The red dashed line is the certified value of the reference soil (400 ppm), not the average of the readings.

Accurate on average, but not precise. The readings average exactly 400, but any single one can be off by 40 ppm or more.

Neither. The readings are scattered and their average (440 ppm) is too high.

Precise and accurate. The readings agree with each other and with the certified value.

Precise, but not accurate. The readings agree closely, but all of them are about 32 ppm too high. This is a bias, like the known biases mentioned at the start. Because it is consistent, it can be measured and corrected.

These four analyzers illustrate three ideas that are easy to confuse:

  • Precision: how finely or consistently something can be measured. Precise readings agree with each other.
  • Accuracy: how close a measurement is to the true value. Accurate readings agree with the truth.
  • Uncertainty: the range of plausible values, and the sources of error, associated with a measurement or its interpretation.

Analyzer D shows why the difference matters: precision alone can’t tell you whether a measurement is right.

Key takeaway 2.1

Precision is agreement between readings; accuracy is agreement with the true value. A precise instrument can still be off, which is why instruments are checked against reference materials.

2.2 How many digits?

The testing company’s spreadsheet reports the average for JEFFERSON ELEM. as:

420.250000 ppm

The analyzer’s nominal precision is ± 20 ppm per reading.

Your turn! How would you report this value to the city council?

Why it matters

Six decimal places don’t add information; they add false precision. A council member reading “420.250000 ppm” could reasonably assume the lead content is known to a millionth of a ppm, and that it is certainly above 400. Neither is true.

The number of digits reported should reflect the precision of the measurement, not the number of digits produced by software or a spreadsheet.

Key takeaway 2.2

Report only the digits that the measurement supports. Extra digits produced by software are false precision: they suggest knowledge that doesn’t exist.

2.3 How precise is the average? (optional)

Go deeper: how averaging narrows the uncertainty (about 5 minutes)

The 420 ppm for JEFFERSON ELEM. is the average of four replicate readings:

Reading 1 2 3 4 Mean Standard deviation
Lead (ppm) 392 447 403 439 420 27

The readings themselves vary, so the average is uncertain too. If the readings are independent (each one a separate, fresh measurement) and all have the same distribution (often a Gaussian - a reasonable assumption), the uncertainty of the mean is the standard error of the mean:

\[\text{SEM} = \frac{\text{standard deviation}}{\sqrt{\text{number of readings}}}\]

For JEFFERSON ELEM., that is 27 / √4 = 13.5 ppm.

A common rule of thumb is that the true value most likely lies within 2 SEM of the mean (about 95% of the time).

Your turn! Is 420 − 2 × 13.5 above the 400 ppm threshold? In other words, can we be confident that JEFFERSON ELEM.’s soil is above the threshold?

How many readings would it take? Move the slider to see how the uncertainty shrinks as you add readings (assuming the mean and standard deviation stay the same).

Two important caveats

Independence. The SEM formula assumes each reading is a fresh, independent measurement. Four readings taken without moving the analyzer, or from the same handful of soil, are not independent: they will agree more closely than separate samples would, and the SEM will make you overconfident.

Bias. More readings shrink the random scatter, but not a bias. Averaging a thousand readings from Analyzer D would give 432 ppm with a tiny SEM: very precise, and still wrong. Only calibration against a reference soil reveals that.

And with WASHINGTON ELEM.’s single reading, you can’t compute a standard deviation at all, so there is no way to tell how uncertain it is. That is one reason why procedures require replicates.

(Statisticians would note that with only a few readings, the plausible range is even wider than 2 SEM. We keep things simple here.)

Key takeaway 2.3

Averaging independent readings narrows the uncertainty of the mean. But it can’t remove a bias, and it only works if the readings really are independent.

2.4 More than the instrument

The analyzer’s ± 20 ppm is only one source of uncertainty. Uncertainty can arise at every stage between the soil in the playground and the number in the spreadsheet.

Your turn! Which of these could affect the 420 ppm reported for JEFFERSON ELEM.? Select all that apply, then reveal our take.

Each reading is only good to ± 20 ppm.

If the correction for the analyzer’s known bias is off, every reading shifts together, and the replicates won’t reveal it.

The readings vary by 27 ppm, more than the analyzer’s ± 20 ppm. The soil itself is uneven.

Wet or unsieved soil reads differently from dry, sieved soil. And whether a value is per kilogram of wet or dry soil changes the number, as you saw in Metadata for Reuse.

Which readings were kept, how the bias was corrected, and how the average was taken all change the final number.

A value in % read as ppm (remember ROOSEVELT HIGH?) is off by a factor of 10,000.

Four readings from one spot may not represent a whole playground. More on this in Activity 3.

Lead in soil can change after renovation work, new mulch, or heavy rain. More on this in Activity 3 too.

Key takeaway 2.4

The instrument is only one source of uncertainty. Other uncertainties can enter at every step between the soil and the spreadsheet: sampling, preparation, calibration, processing, and reporting.

Key takeaway 2

A reported number is only as meaningful as its significant digits, i.e. its uncertainty. Report only the digits the measurement supports, state the uncertainty, and remember that the instrument is rarely the only source of uncertainty.

3. Are the data adequate to the purpose?

About 10 minutes

Following Activity 2, the lab took ten readings from the JEFFERSON ELEM. sample instead of four. It now reports 420 ± 17 ppm: precise enough to say, with confidence, that this soil is above 400 ppm.

That soil came from one spot, about 2 meters from the school building’s wall, collected on 3 March 2025.

3.1 One measurement, four questions

Your turn! Is this one measurement sufficient to answer each of these questions?

What was the lead content of the soil at that spot, on that day?

Enough. This is exactly what was measured.

What is the lead content across JEFFERSON ELEM.’s playground?

Not enough. Lead in soil can vary a lot over a few meters: it tends to be highest next to old painted walls and busy streets. One spot can’t tell you about the rest of the playground. This is a question of spatial representativeness.

Will the playground still be above 400 ppm next school year?

Not enough. Rain, new mulch, landscaping, or dust from renovation work can all change the lead at the surface. A measurement from March describes March. This is a question of temporal representativeness.

Do schools across the district meet the lead standard?

Not enough. One sample from one school can’t describe dozens of schools. The question is at a much larger scale than the measurement (a scale mismatch), and answering it would take many more samples (a higher sampling density).

The measurement never changed. What changed was the claim that could be made from it:

  • Spatial representativeness: how well a measurement at one place stands for a larger area.
  • Temporal representativeness: how well a measurement at one time stands for a longer period.
  • Sampling density: how many samples are taken, over how much space or time.
  • Scale mismatch: when the question is about a much larger (or smaller) area or period than the measurements cover.

Key takeaway 3.1

The same measurement can simultaneously answer one question well and another not at all. Metadata help others figure out which claims they could make (or not) by re-using your data.

Your turn! So, is JEFFERSON ELEM.’s playground above the 400 ppm threshold?

Compare with our answer

It depends on the question.

  • On average, no. Averaged over the whole playground, the soil contains about 270 ppm of lead, well below 400 ppm.
  • Along the building, yes. Every square next to the wall is above 400 ppm, probably because of old lead paint. If children play there, that is the number that matters.
  • Along the road, elevated but below the threshold. The squares next to the road (300–340 ppm) are about twice the playground’s typical level, from decades of leaded-gasoline exhaust.

The original sample was precise and accurate, but it came from a hotspot, so it overstated the playground as a whole. Four squares chosen at random could just as easily miss the hotspot entirely, and understate the risk.

Good sampling follows the question. To estimate the average, spread samples evenly. To find hotspots, also sample where lead is likely to collect: next to old buildings, along streets, and where soil is bare.

Key takeaway 3.2

Where you sample affects what you can conclude. Fit the sampling to the question: spread samples out to estimate an average, and target likely sources to find hotspots.

Key takeaway 3

A measurement can be analytically precise and still be poorly representative of what we want to know. Before using a value, ask what you are trying to get from it: does it cover the place, the time, and the scale of your question? Conversely, when reporting a value, be clear about what questions the data can address.

4. How would you report this value?

About 11 minutes, plus 4 optional

So far, you have been reading values that someone else reported. This time, you write the report.

The testing company has just sampled the playground soil at a new school, ADAMS ELEM. It used Analyzer D, the precise but biased analyzer from Activity 2. On the reference soil, Analyzer D reads 432 ppm instead of 400 ppm, so the company subtracts 32 ppm from every reading. The reference soil’s certificate states its lead content as 400 ± 10 ppm: even the standard has an uncertainty.

Raw (ppm) 458 471 446 467 453 459 10
Corrected, −32 (ppm) 426 439 414 435 421 427 10

Key takeaway 4.1

Every step between raw readings and a reported value, such as a bias correction or an average, is a choice. A good report makes those choices visible.

4.2 What does the ± mean?

Three colleagues summarized the same five readings in three different ways:

427 ± 10 ppm   ·   427 ± 5 ppm   ·   427 ± 14 ppm

Your turn! Which one is correct?

Where does ± 14 come from?

Two independent sources of uncertainty contribute to the average:

  • the scatter between the readings (the standard error of the mean, about 5 ppm);
  • the bias correction, which is only as good as the reference soil’s certificate (about 5 ppm).

Independent uncertainties don’t simply add up; some of the time they partly cancel. Combined, they amount to about 7 ppm, and twice that gives an approximate 95% range of ± 14 ppm. You don’t need to do this calculation yourself; the point is that the reported ± should say which sources it includes.

Key takeaway 4.2

A ± means nothing unless the report says what it is: a standard deviation, a standard error, or a range that also covers other sources of uncertainty.

4.3 What goes in the report?

Some uncertainties can be put into the ± number. Others can’t be, but the reader still needs to know about them.

Go deeper: sort six uncertainties (about 4 minutes)

Your turn! For each of these, decide whether it belongs in the ± number, or should be stated in words.

The five readings differ from each other.

In the ± number. The scatter gives the standard error of the mean, about 5 ppm.

The bias correction (−32 ppm) is itself uncertain.

In the ± number. The reference soil is only certified to ± 10 ppm, so the correction could be off by a few ppm. Unlike the scatter, this error shifts all five readings together, so taking more readings can’t shrink it.

Each reading is only good to ± 20 ppm (the analyzer’s nominal precision).

Already in the ± number. The analyzer’s imprecision is one reason the five readings scatter. Adding it again would count it twice.

The sample came from one spot, by the fence along Oak Street.

In words. No ± can tell how well one spot represents the whole playground (Activity 3). Say where the sample was taken, and that it is a single spot.

The sample was collected in March.

In words. The date tells the reader what period the value describes.

The soil was dried and sieved before analysis.

In words. This method choice affects the value. Stating it lets others compare it with labs that prepare soil differently.

Your turn! The council wants to know whether children are safe in ADAMS ELEM.’s playground. Which of these uncertainties matters most for that question?

The answer depends on the question. If the company wanted to know whether Analyzer D is working properly, the calibration would matter most, and the sampling location not at all.

Key takeaway 4.3

Put what you can quantify in the ±, and state the rest in words. Which uncertainty matters most depends on the question being asked.

Your turn! Look back at your draft from 4.1. What would you add or change? Write your revised report below.

  • The ± is an approximate 95% range. It includes the scatter between readings and the uncertainty of the bias correction.

This report doesn’t settle whether the playground is safe. But it tells the council what is known, how well, and what isn’t known yet. Your report doesn’t need to match ours word for word; what matters is that a reader could tell what the ± means and what the value represents.

Key takeaway 4.4

A good report provides ample context for a reader to interpret a value without having to ask follow up questions like: What was measured? How was it measured? How precisely/accurately? What is not captured by the reported value?

Key takeaway 4

An uncertainty report is only useful if readers know what it includes. Report the digits the measurement supports, say what the ± means and which sources it covers, and state in words the uncertainties that can’t be put in a number, such as where and when the sample was taken. Which uncertainty matters most depends on the question for which the data are used.

5.2 Reputation or evidence?

Your turn! Is each of these a matter of reputation, or of evidence?

The lab is famous.

Reputation. A reasonable first impression, but it says nothing about this particular measurement.

The lab is a government agency.

Reputation (authority). Agencies make mistakes too. Good ones document their work, so that you can check it.

You have used this lab for years without problems.

Reputation (familiarity). Familiarity can hide unstated assumptions: remember the senior employee in Activity 1?

The raw readings and processing notes are archived.

Evidence. Anyone can trace the reported value back to the original observations, and reproduce it.

The analyzer was checked against a reference soil.

Evidence. It shows that the instrument read accurately that day.

The uncertainty is stated, along with what it covers.

Evidence. It lets you judge whether the value is good enough for your question.

Reputation isn’t irrelevant: a lab with a long track record has earned some initial confidence. But reputation, authority and familiarity are reasons to expect good data. Transparent provenance, documented methods, calibration, quality control, ancillary evidence, stated uncertainty and reproducibility are reasons to believe them.

Key takeaway 5.2

Reputation may influence initial confidence, while transparent provenance and documented evidence provide a stronger basis for evaluating data quality.

Key takeaway 5

Trust data because of the evidence that comes with them. When you share your own data, provide that evidence, so that others don’t have to rely on your reputation.

Wrapping up

Data quality is not a universal property of a dataset. It depends on how the data were produced, what evidence supports them, what uncertainties they contain, and whether they are appropriate for the scientific question being asked.
