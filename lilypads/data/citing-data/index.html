Citing Datasets

Learn how to cite datasets in scientific manuscripts, give credit to data creators, and identify the specific data used in your research.
Home › LilyPads › Data › 6. Citing Data in Manuscripts

Datasets are research products in their own right. Just like scientific papers, software, and workflows, they deserve proper attribution. Citing data gives credit to the researchers who collected, processed, and shared them, while helping others find the information behind scientific results.

But citing data isn’t always as straightforward as citing a paper.

What if a dataset has an associated scientific publication or a data paper? Should you cite the paper, the dataset, or both? What if the dataset has been updated since you downloaded it? And how should you reference data that you collected yourself?

In this LilyPad, you’ll explore two common situations:

  1. Citing someone else’s data: How do you acknowledge the original researchers, distinguish between related publications and the dataset itself, and identify the data you actually used?
  2. Citing your own data: How do you make your dataset citable and reference it as your manuscript moves from submission to publication?

By the end, you’ll know how to incorporate data citations into a scientific manuscript, including its reference list and Data Availability Statement, and why citing the right dataset matters.

1. What exactly are you citing?

Imagine you’re studying how agricultural land use affects nitrate concentrations in rivers. You find an existing dataset containing measurements collected over several decades.

Alongside the dataset, you discover two publications:

  • A scientific paper investigating how nitrate concentrations have changed over time and what might explain those changes.
  • A data paper documenting how the measurements were collected, processed, and quality-controlled.

You also find the dataset itself, archived in a repository where you can access the measurements and their metadata.

These three research products serve different purposes.

Research product What does it provide?
Scientific paper Scientific questions, methods, analyses, and interpretations.
Data paper Detailed information about the dataset’s creation, quality, and potential for reuse.
Dataset The actual measurements, associated metadata, and information needed to identify and retrieve the data.

A data paper is a scientific publication focused on describing a dataset rather than primarily presenting new scientific interpretations. Not every dataset has a data paper, and sometimes a single publication combines several of these roles.

Why does the distinction matter?

Suppose you use the nitrate measurements to develop your own model.

Citing the scientific paper acknowledges the scientific work you build upon. Citing the data paper acknowledges the researchers’ work in creating and documenting the dataset. Citing the dataset itself identifies the research product you actually used.

These citations are complementary, not mutually exclusive. Depending on your research, you may need to cite more than one.

What if the dataset changes?

Just like scientific workflows, datasets can evolve. Measurements may be corrected, additional observations included, or metadata improved.

As we explored in the LilyPad on databases, identifying the version you used is important for reproducibility.

When a repository provides a DOI for a specific dataset version, cite that version. Other repositories may provide a single DOI or different mechanisms for identifying releases. Follow the repository’s recommended citation and include the relevant version information when available.

TipTwo questions to remember

When citing data, ask yourself:

  1. Whose work am I building upon? — Give credit to the researchers who collected, created, documented, or interpreted the data.
  2. Which data did I actually use? — Identify the dataset and, when possible, the specific version underlying your results.

2. Citing someone else’s data

The scenario: Studying nitrate in a river

You’re investigating how agricultural land use affects nitrate concentrations in the fictional Alder River watershed.

Rather than collecting new measurements, you find a long-term monitoring dataset containing nitrate observations from 12 stations between 2010 and 2024.

You also discover two publications associated with the monitoring program.

You’re now preparing the Methods section of your manuscript. Which research products should you cite, and where?

One appropriate citation plan

Long-term monitoring in the Alder River watershed has revealed associations between agricultural expansion and increasing nitrate concentrations (Nguyen et al., 2025). To investigate these trends, we analyzed monthly nitrate measurements collected at 12 monitoring stations between 2010 and 2024 (Alder River Monitoring Team, 2025). Quality-control flags were interpreted following the monitoring program's published sampling and validation procedures (Rivera et al., 2025).

Why these citations?

  • Nguyen et al. supports the scientific interpretation connecting agriculture with nitrate trends.
  • Alder River Monitoring Team identifies the actual dataset used in the analysis.
  • Rivera et al. documents how the measurements were collected and validated.

Notice that the scientific paper, data paper, and dataset serve different purposes. In an actual manuscript, multiple citations may be appropriate for a statement. Here we have selected the most directly relevant reference for each one.

What if the repository doesn’t provide version-specific DOIs?

The example above is relatively straightforward because both versions have their own persistent identifiers. But repositories handle dataset updates differently.

Some provide a DOI for each archived version. Others provide a single DOI, use different identifiers, or do not maintain separately retrievable versions.

There is no universal DOI scheme across data repositories.

The goal is to identify the dataset you actually analyzed as precisely as possible.

TipWhen a version-specific DOI isn’t available

Follow the repository’s recommended citation, and include any available version, release, or snapshot information.

For datasets that change over time without preserved versions, document the access date and relevant details of the data extraction, such as query parameters or the temporal and spatial subset used.

A citation may identify the source without guaranteeing that someone can retrieve the exact earlier state of a changing dataset. This is why preserving reproducible snapshots, when possible, matters.

Remember: Cite the scientific or data paper when it supports your methods or provides important context, and cite the dataset to identify the actual data used in your analysis. These are complementary practices.

3. Citing your own data

So far, you’ve used data collected by other researchers. But what happens when you’re the one producing the dataset?

Let’s follow a graduate student who collected new nitrate measurements during storm events in the Alder River watershed and is preparing a manuscript for an AGU journal.

The scenario: From conference poster to published paper

The dataset changes throughout the research project.

Version 0 — The AGU poster

The student presents preliminary results from several sampling stations. Discussions at the conference lead to additional quality-control checks.

Version 1 — Manuscript submission

The student finishes processing the observations and submits a manuscript. The data supporting the submitted results are ready, but the dataset has not yet been made public.

Version 2 — Accepted manuscript

Reviewers request corrections and additional analyses. The student produces a revised dataset that supports the final figures and conclusions.

These are working version labels for our fictional study. They don’t necessarily correspond to archived releases or DOIs.

What should you take away?

For an AGU manuscript, data must be available for peer review, but they do not necessarily need to be public at submission.

Review-only access can be arranged through a repository offering confidential sharing or through the journal’s dedicated peer-review upload option. Neither should be confused with permanent archiving.

By publication, the manuscript should identify the preserved dataset through an appropriate citation and Data Availability Statement.

AGU’s Data and Software for Authors guidance provides the detailed requirements. Always check the policies of the journal to which you’re submitting.

But what does your funder require?

Your preferred data-sharing strategy may not be the one your funder allows.

For example, the NSF Directorate for Geosciences requires final datasets and related research products to be made publicly accessible upon publication or within two years of collection, whichever comes first.

This means a researcher may have to release a dataset before publishing the scientific paper that describes or analyzes it.

More generally, NSF requires data supporting funded publications to be publicly accessible at publication, with justified exceptions documented in the Data Management and Sharing Plan.

There is a real tension here:

  • Sharing early supports transparency, reuse, and timely access to publicly funded research.
  • Waiting until publication gives researchers time to complete their analyses and receive recognition for their work, potentially reducing the risk of being scooped.
  • Funder and journal requirements may limit when and how long data can remain nonpublic.
TipThere is no one-size-fits-all answer

Before deciding when to make your data public, consider your journal’s requirements, your funder’s policies, your project’s Data Management and Sharing Plan, and the repository’s available access controls.

These policies continue to evolve. Always check the requirements that apply to your particular research project.

What happened to version 1?

If version 1 was already publicly archived, it should remain identifiable as an earlier release. The researcher should not silently replace it with different measurements.

If version 1 was only shared privately during peer review, that review copy does not necessarily need to become a separate public dataset. What matters for the published paper is that the final data supporting its results are properly preserved and cited.

What should you take away?

A dataset citation is more than a formality added at submission. It must remain consistent with the data supporting the published results.

Before publication, check that:

  • The final dataset is preserved in an appropriate repository.
  • The reference list cites the correct dataset and version, when available.
  • The Data Availability Statement explains how readers can access it.

The AGU Data and Software for Authors guidelines provide examples of dataset citations and Availability Statements.

4. Before you submit your paper…

Whether you’re using someone else’s data or publishing your own, citing datasets is about more than providing a link. It’s about giving credit to the people who produced the data and helping others identify the measurements behind your scientific results.

Before submitting your next manuscript, ask yourself:

  • Am I giving credit? Have I cited the datasets I used, along with relevant scientific publications or data papers?
  • Am I citing the right data? Do my references identify the actual datasets and versions used in my analysis?
  • Can someone access the data? Are the data available to reviewers, and have I planned for their preservation and accessibility at publication?
  • Have I included a Data Availability Statement? Does it explain where readers can find the data, including identifiers and access conditions?
  • Have I checked the final citations? If the data changed during peer review, do my references and Data Availability Statement reflect the data supporting the published results?
TipOne last thing to remember

A dataset citation and a Data Availability Statement serve complementary purposes.

  • The citation gives credit to the data creators and identifies the dataset used in your research.
  • The Data Availability Statement tells readers where and how they can access the data.

A scientific paper or data paper may provide valuable context, but it doesn’t replace the citation to the data themselves.

And remember: the latest dataset isn’t necessarily the one you used!
