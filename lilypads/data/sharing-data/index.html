Preparing data for sharing and reuse

Home › LilyPads › Data › 5. Preparing Data for Sharing and Reuse

Sharing a dataset is an important step toward open science, but making data available does not necessarily make them reusable. In this LilyPad, you will explore how different researchers interpret the same dataset, discover what happens when important information is not documented, and consider how much documentation is enough for meaningful reuse. You will also learn how community conventions can help make data understandable beyond the original research team, while recognizing the time and effort required to prepare data for sharing.

1. Who can reuse this dataset?

Imagine that your research group has been studying water quality in a river. The team collected water samples at different locations to investigate whether nitrate concentrations differ upstream and downstream of a developed section of the river.

The measurements are stored in a CSV file named stream_nitrate_2025.csv.

date site NO3_mgL qc
2025-05-14 S01 0.42 1
2025-05-14 S03 1.18 1
2025-06-12 S01 0.47 1
2025-06-12 S03 1.31 2

This is a fictional dataset created for this activity. Only four illustrative rows are shown.

At first glance, the file looks straightforward. You can read it, and several column names seem familiar. But look a little closer:

  • What do S01 and S03 represent?
  • What do you think NO3 means?
  • What do the values in qc tell you?

You might feel certain about your answers. But would that be enough to conduct the analysis? For example, if you want to compare upstream and downstream sites, can you locate them from the site codes alone?

Let’s see how three researchers make sense of the same file, each bringing a different kind of background knowledge.

What does Taylor bring?

Probably already knows

  • How to combine observations with geographic information.

Still needs

  • What nitrate measures and how to interpret its values.
  • The meaning of quality flags and important study limitations.

Notice: Reuse across fields may need additional explanations. A dataset does not, however, need to teach every reader an entire discipline.

What changed?

The file stayed exactly the same. What changed was the knowledge each person could bring to interpreting it. Some knowledge is written down; some is shared informally or assumed. This unrecorded understanding is called tacit knowledge.

Alex knows the sites from fieldwork, but might not know another colleague’s private QC shorthand. Sam knows water chemistry, but not this lab’s site codes. Taylor knows spatial analysis, but may need more context to interpret the measurements.

Whose conventions are we using?

A convention is an agreed or habitual way to name, describe, or organize information. Explore how far different conventions might be understood.

Individual: A researcher may invent a QC code or file-naming rule that makes perfect sense to them. Without an explanation, even their lab colleagues may be unsure what it means.

Lab: Everyone in the group may know that S01 is an upstream site. An outside researcher still needs a site table or map to make sense of it.

Field: A water-quality community may use shared terminology, recommended units, and standard ways to describe measurements and quality flags. These practices make exchange easier, but the dataset must still identify which conventions it follows.

Widely shared: Formats such as CSV and recognizable dates such as 2025-05-14 help many researchers and tools read a file. They do not, on their own, explain the scientific meaning of its columns.

Key takeaway: The farther someone is from the original research context, the more of that context may need to be explicit. Conventions help when the people exchanging data share them; documenting or using broader community conventions makes reuse less dependent on inside knowledge. The goal is enough context for intended reuse, not an explanation of everything to everyone.

What would make this a stronger handoff?

The folder isn't useless. It already contains an informative README, a site lookup, and an identifiable revised data table. But Alex still cannot establish some important facts without guessing.

A short data dictionary defining the measurement and its units, a clear QC key, explanations of revisions and exclusions, and an explicit reuse statement would address the biggest gaps. Better filenames and a clear distinction between raw, processed, and presentation-only files would also help.

Why conventions matter: Morgan's personal QC shorthand worked for Morgan, but not necessarily for Alex. Lab or field conventions can help people share a common understanding—provided the dataset identifies which conventions it follows and explains departures from them.

Key takeaway: Being able to open a dataset does not mean you can use it confidently. A reusable dataset should not depend on “ask the person who made it.”

What does each package make possible?

For Sam's task, B is the practical middle ground. A leaves consequential gaps; C adds substantial value for other potential uses, but much of it goes beyond what Sam needs for this comparison. More documentation is not automatically better if the cost of producing it outweighs its likely reuse value.

What is the practical middle ground? For Sam's comparison, the second package is enough: it identifies the nitrate measurement, sites, QC rules, revisions, and reuse conditions using shared conventions. The third package adds considerable detail that may be more than this particular analysis requires, though it could enable other kinds of reuse.

Existing field conventions can reduce the work of deciding what to record. For water-quality data, the US EPA's Water Quality eXchange (WQX) templates illustrate commonly captured information such as the measured characteristic, units, sample fraction, analytical method, and location. Our fictional package borrows these ideas; it is not presented as a WQX-ready submission.

4. Which research products should you preserve?

So far, we’ve focused on the documentation needed to make a dataset reusable. But scientific research often produces more than one potentially valuable data product. Which ones are worth preserving and sharing?

Consider three examples:

  • A benthic isotope stack — the primary research product. Researchers combine records from multiple sediment cores to build a new stack. The stack is a scientific contribution in its own right and is archived as a new dataset.
  • New age models — related research products. The researchers develop new age models for individual cores while building the stack. Those models may be useful for other scientific questions, and their authorship or provenance may differ from the stack’s. It may make sense to preserve them separately and link them to the stack.
  • Time series extracted from NetCDF files — computational intermediates. A researcher extracts a few time series from large climate-model outputs for an analysis. The extracts may not be independent scientific contributions, but retaining them could make the analysis easier to reproduce or spare others a costly extraction. If they are easy to regenerate from accessible source files and a documented workflow, preserving separate copies may offer little benefit.

How would you decide? Consider whether another researcher could reuse the product, whether it would help reproduce the analysis, how much effort or computation recreating it would require, whether it has distinct provenance or authorship, and whether an equivalent product is already available elsewhere.

There is no universal rule. Not every intermediate file needs to be archived or assigned its own DOI. Some products may be kept with the main dataset, some may be worth sharing separately, and others can be recreated from well-documented inputs and workflows. The choice should reflect their scientific and practical value.

Want to learn how to manage and publish these products? An upcoming Deep End LilyPad will explore the practical steps. You can find it through The Deep End when it is available.

Key takeaway

Making data reusable means documenting enough of their scientific context that the intended users can understand and work with them correctly. Community conventions provide a practical starting point, without requiring every project to create the most exhaustive metadata package imaginable. It also means thinking deliberately about which related or intermediate products would be valuable to preserve.
