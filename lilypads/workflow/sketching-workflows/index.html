Sketching Workflows

A scientific workflow is more than a list of tasks. It shows how the different parts of a scientific process fit together — including data collection, preprocessing, analysis, decisions, parameter choices, and outputs. In this activity, you will practice turning a short scientific description into a visual workflow.

Choose a scenario

Choose one of the scenarios below based on the field that feels most familiar or interesting to you. You do not need specialized knowledge of the topic, and you only need to complete one scenario.

For your chosen scenario, you will:
1. identify the major steps in the scientific process,
2. create and arrange those steps on a canvas,
3. connect them to show how the workflow progresses,
4. include important choices or parameter values needed for reproducibility,
5. compare your workflow with the answer.

Choose one scenario to complete:

  • Hydrology
  • Seismology
  • Oceanography
  • Geophysics

Scenario: Water Quality

Scientific question:
Does water quality change as a stream flows past a developed area?

A research team collects water samples from several stations upstream and downstream of a developed area. Each sample is labeled with its station number and collection date, and the team measures its nitrate concentration. Their measurement method is valid for nitrate concentrations between 0 and 50 mg/L.

Before analyzing the data, they:

  • convert all nitrate measurements to the same units,
  • average duplicate measurements collected at the same station on the same date,
  • remove measurements without a completed quality-control check,
  • discard nitrate values greater than 50 mg/L, outside the valid measurement range.

They then calculate the average nitrate concentration at each station and plot how nitrate levels change along the stream.

Scenario: Earthquake frequency

Scientific question:

Where are earthquakes occurring most frequently in a given region?

A research team uses an earthquake catalog containing the event identifier, date and time, location, and magnitude of each recorded earthquake. The catalog is considered complete for earthquakes of magnitude 2.0 or greater between 2015 and 2024.

Before analyzing the data, they:

  • remove duplicate records with the same event identifier,
  • remove records without a latitude or longitude,
  • retain earthquakes recorded between 2015 and 2024,
  • discard earthquakes with magnitudes below 2.0.

They then divide the region into 10 × 10 km grid cells, count the earthquakes within each cell, and create a map showing the number of earthquakes across the region.

Scenario: Ocean thermal structure

Scientific question:

How does ocean temperature change with depth?

A research team collects several ocean temperature profiles at the same location. Each measurement includes the profile identifier, depth, temperature, and a quality-control flag.

Before analyzing the data, they:

  • convert all depths to meters,
  • convert all temperatures to degrees Celsius,
  • remove measurements without a completed quality-control check from the QA column (0 = valid, 1 = invalid),
  • retain measurements from the upper 500 meters of the ocean.

They then divide the water column into 10-meter depth intervals, calculate the average temperature within each interval, and plot average temperature against depth.

Scenario: Ground motion

Scientific question:

Is the ground moving in this area over time?

A research team uses daily position measurements from several GNSS stations, which are high-precision geodetic monitoring sites permanently anchored to bedrock or stable ground to measure tectonic and surface displacement over time. Each measurement includes the station identifier, date, east–west and north–south position, measurement uncertainty, and a quality-control flag.

Before analyzing the data, they:

  • convert all positions to a common reference frame and units,
  • remove measurements without a completed quality-control check,
  • discard measurements with horizontal uncertainty greater than 10 mm,
  • retain stations with at least three years of observations.

For each remaining station, they fit linear trends to the east–west and north–south positions over time. They combine the two trends to calculate the speed and direction of ground movement and plot the resulting velocity arrows on a map.

Sketch your workflow

Turn the scientific process above into a workflow.

Create as many steps as you think you need, arrange them on the canvas, and connect them to show how information moves through the analysis. Each step should include an input data (with optional parameters), an analysis, and an output. Intermediate outputs can be used as inputs for later steps.

Remember: Include choices and parameter values that someone would need to reproduce the workflow. For example, don’t just write remove invalid values if the description tells you exactly what counts as invalid.

Add components to your workflow and describe each one. You can move components around the canvas by dragging the ⠿ handle. Turn Connect on, then click the component an arrow should start from followed by the component it should point to.

Add data, analysis steps, and parameters to begin.

Sofware vs. Workflows - Revisited

In a previous LiLyPad, we discussed the difference between software and workflows. Form this exercise, you should have a better understanding how workflows use software (e.g., the analysis cards) but they represent much more than just that computational step. Workflows also include the data, parameters, and decisions that are used to produce a scientific result.
