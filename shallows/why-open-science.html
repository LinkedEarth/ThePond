Why Open Science?

Evidence and stories to make the case, and answers to common worries

Home › The Shallows › Why Open Science?

Why it matters

Science works because claims can be checked independently. When the data, code, and methods behind a result are open, mistakes are caught sooner, others can build on the work instead of wasting time re-inventing the wheel, and the public has good reason to trust what scientists say. This is the heart of the Center for Open Science’s mission: research that is open, has integrity, and is reproducible.

Open science is good for you, too. Shared work is easier to find, cite, and credit, and it opens doors to new collaborations (McKiernan et al. (2016)).

If you are reading this, you probably need little convincing. This page gives you a few numbers and stories to help you make the case to others, and answers to the worries early-career researchers raise most often (Allen & Mehler (2019)).

Open-access articles receive 18% more citations than average (Piwowar et al. (2018)).

Articles whose data availability statement links to a repository receive up to 25% more citations (Colavizza et al. (2020)).

The median effect in the replications was 85% smaller than in the original experiments (Errington et al. (2021)). Results that are open can be checked, and checking is how science corrects itself.

“But what about my career?”

Flip at least two of these cards.

Preprints and preregistrations are public, date-stamped records, so they show the idea was yours first. With a Registered Report, a journal accepts your study before you collect the data.

Try: post your next manuscript as a preprint, e.g. on OSF Preprints or a preprint server for your field.

Some practices take time up front, but they pay it back: when a reviewer asks for a new analysis, or when you return to a project a year later, organized and shared code saves you hours. You don’t have to do everything at once.

Try: share the code behind one figure in your next paper.

Registered Reports are accepted for the question and the methods, so they are published whatever the result. Only 44% of Registered Reports in psychology report positive results, versus 96% of standard papers, which suggests the standard literature hides many null results (Scheel et al. (2021)).

Try: check whether a journal in your field offers Registered Reports.

Data and code deposited with a digital object identifier (DOI) can be cited just like a paper, and papers that link their data are cited more (see above). Journals such as the Journal of Open Source Software publish papers about research software.

Try: add a CITATION.cff file to your GitHub repository so others know how to cite it.

Problems open science is solving

  • Psychology’s replication crisis. When 100 psychology studies were repeated, only about a third of the results held up (Open Science Collaboration (2015)). The field responded with preregistration and Registered Reports (see What is Open Science?).
  • A pandemic. The genome of the virus that causes COVID-19 was shared publicly in January 2020 (Wu et al. (2020)), and some vaccine developers were able to begin designing candidates within days.
  • Forests. When the U.S. Geological Survey made the Landsat satellite archive free in 2008, use of the data soared (Wulder et al. (2012)). Free data made the first high-resolution global maps of forest loss possible (Hansen et al. (2013)).

Careers built in the open

  • Thomas Herndon, a PhD student in economics, was assigned to replicate a famous paper on public debt. The authors shared their spreadsheet, and he found errors that changed its conclusions (Herndon et al. (2013)). The finding made international news.
  • Fernando Pérez started IPython as a side project in 2001, while a graduate student. It grew into Project Jupyter, which won the 2017 Software System Award from the Association for Computing Machinery. Jupyter has become a cornerstone of modern research and education in data science, which underlies many computationally based scientific fields.
  • Ed Hawkins, a climate scientist, released his “warming stripes” graphic in 2018 for anyone to use. He has also long shared his code and insights on his blog and substack. The stripes have become one of the most recognizable emblems of climate change, and in 2020 Dr Hawkins was appointed a Member of the Order of the British Empire (a British honor) for services to climate science and science communication.
