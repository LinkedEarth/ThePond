What is Open Science?

The practices that make research visible, reusable, and verifiable

Home › The Shallows › What is Open Science?

A working definition

The Center for Open Science (COS) describes open science as increasing the openness, integrity, and reproducibility of research. Open science (OS) means making the whole research process—questions, plans, data, code, methods, results, and reviews—transparent and accessible, so that others can see how a finding was produced, check it, and build on it.

A broader definition comes from the UNESCO Recommendation on Open Science (2021), adopted by 193 countries. It describes OS as making scientific knowledge openly available, accessible, and reusable for everyone, and opening up the processes of creating, evaluating, and communicating that knowledge to society beyond the traditional scientific community.

Open science, thus, is not a single thing. It is a set of practices, and you can adopt them one at a time.

Definition. Anyone can read research results, with no paywall.

Examples.

  • Physics & beyond: arXiv has made preprints free to read since 1991.
  • Geoscience: EarthArXiv does the same for Earth sciences.

Definition. Data are shared publicly, promptly, and with enough documentation for others to reuse them.

Examples.

  • Genomics: the Human Genome Project’s Bermuda Principles (1996) required DNA sequences to be released within 24 hours.
  • Oceanography: the Argo network of ~4,000 robotic floats publishes ocean temperature and salinity data openly, within a day of collection (Wong et al. (2020)).

Definition. Data are Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable—by people and machines. FAIR is about usability, not only availability (Wilkinson et al. (2016)).

Examples.

  • Biology: the Protein Data Bank has shared standardized protein structures since 1971 (Berman et al. (2000)); this archive made it possible to train AlphaFold (Jumper et al. (2021)).
  • Climate: CMIP6 climate model output follows common metadata conventions, so one script can compare dozens of models (Eyring et al. (2016)).

Definition. The code behind the results is public, licensed, and reusable.

Examples.

  • Astrophysics: LIGO’s first detection of gravitational waves (Abbott et al. (2016)) came with open data and code at the Gravitational Wave Open Science Center, so anyone can reproduce the signal.
  • Seismology: ObsPy is a community Python toolbox for processing seismic data (Beyreuther et al. (2010)).

Definition. The full path from raw data to figures—scripts, notebooks, computing environments—is shared so it can be rerun.

Examples.

  • Public health: Nextstrain tracked SARS-CoV-2 evolution in real time with open, rerunnable workflows (Hadfield et al. (2018)).
  • Geoscience: Pangeo is a community building open, cloud-ready workflows for big Earth data.

Definition. Hypotheses and analysis plans are recorded before results are known, which keeps planned (confirmatory) and unplanned (exploratory) analyses distinct. See COS: Preregistration and Registered Reports.

Example.

  • Medicine: after large NHLBI-funded clinical trials had to declare their outcomes in advance on ClinicalTrials.gov (from 2000), the share reporting a benefit fell from 57% to 8% (Kaplan & Irvin (2015)).

Definition. Others can obtain the same results from the same data and code (reproducibility), or confirm a finding with new data (replication).

Example.

  • Psychology: the COS-led Reproducibility Project repeated 100 published studies; 97% of the originals reported significant results, but only 36% of the replications did (Open Science Collaboration (2015)).

Definition. Reviews, and the authors’ responses, are published alongside the paper.

Examples.

  • Life sciences: eLife publishes its reviewers’ assessments with every paper.
  • Geoscience: Copernicus/EGU journals post submissions for public discussion, and keep the reviews and replies online.

Definition. Shared, community-governed tools to store, identify, and link research outputs.

Examples.

  • General: the COS Open Science Framework (OSF), Zenodo (free DOIs for data and code), and ORCID (a persistent ID for every researcher).
  • Earth science: NASA Earthdata provides open access to decades of satellite observations.

Definition. Teaching materials that are free to use, adapt, and share.

Examples.

  • Computing: The Carpentries teach coding and data skills with openly licensed lessons.
  • Geoscience: Project Pythia offers open tutorials for geoscience computing. The Pond itself is an open educational resource.

Definition. People outside academia take part in the research itself.

Examples.

  • Biochemistry: players of the online game Foldit solved the structure of a retroviral protein that had stumped scientists for over a decade (Khatib et al. (2011)).
  • Climate: Old Weather volunteers transcribe historical ship logbooks, recovering weather observations from the 19th and 20th centuries.

Definition. Journals, funders, and institutions set standards that reward openness.

Examples.

  • General: the COS TOP Guidelines give journals a menu of transparency standards (Nosek et al. (2015)); after Psychological Science adopted COS Open Science Badges, articles reporting open data rose from under 3% to 39% (Kidwell et al. (2016)).
  • Earth science: major publishers and repositories committed to FAIR data in Earth, space, and environmental science (Stall et al. (2019)).

Culture changes

Hopefully, the section above convinced you that there are a few actionable ways in which you can incorporate OS in your own research life. One oft-cited obstacle is that no researcher can single-handedly enact a cultural shift. Single-handedly, no. But together, yes. COS describes a strategy for culture change in five layers, each one building on the preceding layer:

  1. Possible — infrastructure exists (e.g., a repository where you can deposit data).
  2. Easy — tools make the practice simple (e.g., one-click DOIs).
  3. Normative — communities see it as the expected thing to do (e.g., badges make it visible).
  4. Rewarding — incentives recognize it (e.g., hiring and funding credit shared data).
  5. Required — policies mandate it (e.g., funder data-sharing requirements).

Please take a moment to reflect on how these qualifiers apply to the practices that are already part of your research life, and those you want/need to develop.

What open science is not

Misconception In practice Example
“Everything must be public.” Openness is balanced against privacy, safety, and the rights of communities: as open as possible, as closed as necessary. The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance complement FAIR by centering people and purpose (Carroll et al. (2020)).
“If I share, others will take credit for my work.” Licenses and persistent identifiers make credit easier to trace. A dataset with a Zenodo DOI and a CC-BY license can be cited, and the citations counted.
“It’s all or nothing.” Every step counts, even sharing a single script. Start with what is possible/easy for you today.
